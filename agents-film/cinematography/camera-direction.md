# Camera Direction for AI Video Creation

## Purpose

Use this guide when designing or prompting a video shot. The goal is to turn the creative intent into clear, specific visual direction that a video-generation system or production team can execute consistently.

Treat cinematography as visual storytelling. Every shot choice should help the viewer understand the scene, notice the right detail, or feel the intended emotion. Be precise and thorough about details that affect the output. Do not add technical flourishes that do not improve the shot.

## Core rule

**Start with the story intention, then choose the simplest set of visual decisions that expresses it clearly.**

Do not select a shot because its name sounds cinematic. Select it because its framing, perspective, composition, focus, and movement serve a defined purpose. A restrained, stable shot is often stronger than unnecessary camera motion.

## Shot-planning sequence

For each shot, work through these decisions in order:

1. **Narrative purpose:** What should the viewer understand, notice, or feel in this moment?
2. **Subject and action:** Who or what is visible? What exactly happens, and how does the subject interact with the environment?
3. **Shot size:** How much of the subject and setting should be visible?
4. **Camera position and angle:** From where and at what height does the viewer see the action?
5. **Composition:** Where are the important elements placed in the frame, and what attracts attention first?
6. **Camera movement:** Does the camera need to move? If so, how, when, how far, and at what pace?
7. **Focus and depth:** What stays sharp, what falls out of focus, and does focus change?
8. **Continuity and feasibility:** Does the shot match neighboring shots and the production or generation constraints?

Do not force a choice in every category when it is irrelevant. State a deliberate default, such as a locked-off camera, when useful.

## Shot size: choose the viewer's distance

Use shot size to control how much context and emotional detail the viewer receives. Names and exact crop boundaries can vary by convention; describe the visible framing plainly whenever precision matters.

- **Extreme wide / extreme long shot:** The subject is small within a large environment. Use to establish scale, geography, distance, exposure, or isolation.
- **Wide / long shot:** Shows the subject in the setting with substantial environmental context. Use to establish where the action happens or how a subject relates to a place.
- **Full shot:** Shows the subject's full body. Use when posture, physical action, costume, or body language matters while the setting remains legible.
- **Medium-wide / cowboy framing:** Frames roughly from the knees or mid-thigh upward. Use when body language and a relevant prop or interaction need to remain visible.
- **Medium shot:** Commonly frames from the waist upward. Use for dialogue, gestures, and interaction while retaining some context.
- **Medium close-up:** Commonly frames from the chest upward. Use to prioritize a face while preserving some body language and surroundings.
- **Close-up:** Fills much of the frame with a face or important subject. Use to make emotion, reaction, or a key object prominent.
- **Extreme close-up / detail:** Isolates a small feature or object, such as eyes, fingers, a button, or a texture. Use only when that detail carries meaning or action.
- **Establishing shot:** A functional opening view, usually wide, that makes the location and spatial layout understandable. It is defined by its purpose, not by one exact shot size.

Use changes in shot size intentionally. A closer shot can intensify attention or reveal a reaction; a wider shot can restore context or make a subject feel small. Avoid arbitrary shot changes.

## Framing and subject relationships

Describe who is featured and how subjects relate within the image:

- **Single:** One subject is the clear focus, even if another person or object appears indistinctly in the foreground or background.
- **Two-shot / group shot:** Multiple subjects share the frame. Their spacing, posture, relative scale, and eyelines can communicate connection, conflict, distance, or power.
- **Over-the-shoulder (OTS):** Frames one subject from behind another subject's shoulder. Use to orient a conversation or preserve a sense of connection between speakers.
- **Point-of-view (POV):** Shows what a character sees. Make clear whose point of view it is and what the viewer should notice. For a reaction beat, establish the character's look, show the viewed subject or object, then show the reaction when the sequence requires it.

For dialogue and interactions, keep screen direction, eyelines, and relative positions coherent from shot to shot. Do not reverse who is looking where without a motivated change in viewpoint.

## Camera angle and height: choose a perspective

Specify both the camera's height relative to the subject and whether it looks level, up, or down when that distinction matters.

- **Eye-level / neutral:** A familiar, comparatively neutral perspective that supports direct connection with a subject.
- **Low angle:** Camera looks upward from below the subject. It can suggest dominance, threat, grandeur, or vulnerability in the viewer; context determines the actual reading.
- **High angle:** Camera looks down on the subject. It can suggest vulnerability, exposure, distance, or a clear view of spatial relationships.
- **Overhead / bird's-eye:** Camera looks down from nearly above. Use to clarify layout, patterns, movement, or a subject's relation to the environment.
- **Ground-level / knee-level / hip-level / shoulder-level:** Name a specific height when it changes what is visible, the perceived scale, or the character's perspective.
- **Dutch angle / tilted horizon:** Slants the camera's horizon. Use sparingly for deliberate instability, disorientation, or stylization; do not use as generic decoration.

These are expressive tendencies, not fixed meanings. Do not claim that an angle always makes a character powerful, weak, sinister, or innocent. Describe the intended effect and make sure it fits the scene.

## Composition: direct attention

Describe the arrangement of important visual elements in the frame, not just the camera label. Specify as needed:

- Subject placement (center, left, right, foreground, middle ground, or background).
- Headroom, look room, and negative space, especially in the direction a subject looks or moves.
- Whether the subject is isolated or visually connected to other people or objects.
- Relevant foreground and background elements, and whether they should be clear, soft, or unobtrusive.
- The first point of attention and any important information that must remain visible.
- The subject's orientation, eyeline, and screen direction.

Keep the frame readable. Avoid competing focal points, accidental obstructions, awkward cropping, or background detail that distracts from the story. If text, a product, a face, or an action must be legible, state that explicitly.

## Camera movement: motivate and describe it precisely

Keep the camera still unless movement contributes a clear story or visual purpose. When it does move, specify:

- **Type:** locked-off, pan, tilt, dolly/push-in or pull-back, tracking/follow, lateral move, arc/orbit, crane/jib rise or descent, handheld, or zoom.
- **Direction and path:** where the camera starts, where it travels or turns, and what it moves relative to.
- **Timing:** when movement begins, what event motivates it, and when it stops.
- **Pace and feel:** slow and smooth, measured, abrupt, energetic, subtly handheld, and so on.
- **Framing behavior:** whether the subject stays the same size, grows or shrinks in frame, remains centered, or is revealed or concealed.

Distinguish a **dolly/push-in** (the camera physically moves closer) from a **zoom-in** (the lens changes focal length while camera position stays put). Do not use them interchangeably. Avoid vague directions such as “make it dynamic” when a specific move can be described.

Use specialized moves, such as a whip pan or dolly zoom, only when their recognizable effect serves the moment. Do not stack multiple camera moves unless they can be performed clearly and the result benefits from them.

## Focus and depth of field

State what should be in focus and why when focus materially affects the image:

- **Shallow depth of field:** Keeps the subject sharp and softens foreground or background to isolate attention.
- **Deep focus:** Keeps important elements at different distances legible, supporting spatial relationships or layered action.
- **Rack focus:** Deliberately shifts focus from one subject or plane to another to redirect attention or reveal information. Specify the starting and ending subjects and the cue or timing for the shift.
- **Focus tracking:** Keeps a moving subject acceptably sharp as it changes position.

Do not request blur that hides important action, identity, text, or setting information. Do not add a focus pull just to make a shot seem sophisticated.

## Continuity across shots

For a sequence, check that consecutive shots can be understood as the same continuous scene unless a deliberate transition is intended. Preserve:

- Character identity, appearance, clothing, props, and carried objects.
- Location layout, time of day, lighting direction, and weather.
- Subject positions, movement paths, eyelines, and screen direction.
- The camera's side of the action and the apparent geography of the scene.
- The state of objects and actions at the cut (for example, which hand holds an object or whether a door is open).
- A plausible change in framing and viewpoint between neighboring shots.

Use a wide or establishing view when needed to orient the viewer. Use medium and close coverage when it adds useful performance or detail. Do not generate redundant shots that repeat the same information without a storytelling reason.

## Feasibility for video generation and production

Write directions that can be represented clearly in the requested tool or production setup. Consider the clip's duration, aspect ratio, camera controls, motion capability, subject count, and any supplied reference assets. Prefer one clear primary action and one clear camera behavior per shot when complex action risks ambiguity.

Do not specify equipment such as a drone, crane, dolly, or gimbal unless the request concerns a real shoot or the equipment meaningfully describes the desired motion. For generated video, describe the visible camera behavior rather than assuming physical gear is available.

Keep a shot internally consistent. Do not ask the camera to be simultaneously locked off and moving, or the subject to remain fully visible while the framing excludes them. Resolve conflicting instructions before delivering the prompt or shot plan.

## Adapting Camera Direction to Social Media Videos

Social media describes a distribution context, not a single visual style. A vlog, a product demonstration, a short documentary, a comedy sketch, and a talking-head explanation may all be social media videos, but they do not necessarily benefit from the same shots or visual rhythm.

Do not apply a fixed shot recipe to social media videos. Do not assume that every video needs a talking-head medium shot, frequent cuts, close-ups, B-roll, vertical framing, or fast camera movement. These are options to consider, not default requirements.

First understand the specific video:

- What is its purpose, subject, tone, and intended audience?
- What should viewers notice, understand, or feel?
- Is the video primarily direct-to-camera, observational, narrative, instructional, performative, or a combination?
- Which platform, aspect ratio, duration, and viewing context apply, if known?
- What actions, objects, expressions, or setting details need to be seen clearly?

Then choose the shot sizes, angles, compositions, focus, and camera movement that best serve that video. Cinematic techniques may be used whenever they strengthen the intended experience. A restrained setup may be best for a direct conversation; a sequence of varied shots may better serve a demonstration, story, or visual reveal. Neither approach is mandatory.

Treat shot types as a flexible visual vocabulary, not a checklist. For every significant choice, be able to explain what it contributes to this particular video. Avoid adding a shot or camera effect solely to make the video feel more cinematic, energetic, or polished.

When the video brief leaves room for interpretation, make a considered choice based on the available context. Ask a clarifying question only when missing information would materially change the visual approach. Otherwise, state any important assumption briefly and proceed.

The goal is not to prescribe which shots social media videos should use. The goal is to help the agent select and describe the shots that best communicate the specific video being created.

## Recommended shot specification

Use this structure for each shot. Omit fields that do not matter, but retain enough detail to make the shot unambiguous.

```text
Shot [number] — [brief purpose]
Duration: [seconds, if known]
Purpose: [what the viewer should understand, notice, or feel]
Subject and action: [who/what appears; specific, observable action]
Shot size: [framing and visible crop]
Camera position and angle: [height, direction, and perspective]
Composition: [subject placement, spatial relationships, background, and attention point]
Camera movement: [type, direction/path, timing, pace; or locked-off]
Focus: [sharp subject/plane, depth of field, and any focus change]
Continuity / constraints: [details that must match; format, identity, or feasibility needs]
```

## Final quality check

Before delivering a shot or sequence, verify:

1. Every shot has a clear purpose tied to the story or viewer experience.
2. The subject, action, framing, and camera behavior are concrete and observable.
3. The camera movement and focus choices are motivated, feasible, and not contradictory.
4. The composition makes the important information easy to see.
5. The sequence preserves identity, screen direction, eyelines, geography, and action continuity.
6. No decorative technique or redundant shot makes the result harder to execute or understand.
7. The description is detailed enough to guide the output without burying the central visual idea in unnecessary jargon.
