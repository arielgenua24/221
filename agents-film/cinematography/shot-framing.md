# Shot Framing for AI Video Creation

## Purpose

This guide teaches shot framing in three stages: first, understand the framing vocabulary; second, learn what these choices can contribute without treating them as fixed rules; third, adapt them to social media and AI-generated video so the composition remains clear and natural to the specific content.

Framing is the deliberate arrangement of subjects and visual information within the image. It is not a recipe of shot names. Choose a framing because it helps the viewer understand a relationship, follow an action, notice a detail, or experience a point of view.

# Part 1: Learn the framing vocabulary

Framing terms describe different aspects of an image. Some name how many subjects are featured; others describe a foreground relationship, a point of view, or an isolated detail. These categories can overlap in one shot.

## Count of featured subjects

In these terms, count the subjects that are featured as the main subjects of the frame. Do not count every person whose shoulder, head, hand, or silhouette happens to be visible as a featured subject.

- **Single:** One subject is featured. A single can be clean, with no other person visible, or dirty, with a partial or unfocused foreground element from another person or object. A dirty single may still have just one featured subject.
- **Two-shot:** Two subjects are featured together in the frame. Their placement and spacing can be as important as the number of subjects.
- **Three-shot:** Three subjects are featured together. The composition should make their grouping and individual relationships readable.
- **Group shot:** More than three subjects are featured, or a group is treated as the collective subject. State the important people or arrangement when a generic group label is not specific enough.

Here, **single** means one featured subject, not one continuous take. Because “one-shot” can also mean an uninterrupted take, prefer “single” when describing subject count.

## Foreground-framed compositions

- **Over-the-shoulder (OTS):** The camera frames the main subject or target past a shoulder or part of a head in the foreground. Often used with two people in conversation, but the target beyond the shoulder can also be an object, an action, or a space. The person whose shoulder is visible does not have to be a second featured subject.
- **Over-the-hip (OTH):** Similar to OTS, but a hip or part of the lower body forms the foreground frame. The far subject or target may be another person, an object, or a space. If the relative heights or positions matter, describe them rather than assuming the framing automatically communicates a power relationship.

OTS and OTH describe how the foreground relates to the target in the composition. They do not prescribe the number of featured subjects. For example, an OTS can frame one featured person looking at a screen, landscape, or object; it can also frame one featured conversation partner with the other person's shoulder visible in the foreground.

## Point of view and detail framing

- **Point-of-view (POV):** Shows what a specified character or other viewpoint source sees. Identify whose perspective it is and what appears in their view. A POV shot is not the same as merely placing a camera near a character; the image should read as that character's or source's view.
- **Insert shot:** Isolates a specific detail, object, action, or piece of information so the viewer notices it. An insert may show something a character is looking at, but it does not have to be a POV shot.

POV and insert can overlap: a POV may reveal a detail, or an insert may show a detail from an external camera position. They remain distinct ideas: POV identifies whose view is shown; insert identifies the detail being emphasized.

## Composition within the frame

Framing also involves the placement and visual relationship of elements. Consider:

- Which subject is the primary focus and how much of the frame they occupy.
- Where subjects appear relative to one another, and what their spacing or posture communicates.
- Whether foreground, middle-ground, and background elements help orient the viewer or compete for attention.
- How visual weight is distributed across the frame. A balanced image may distribute attention across both sides; an intentionally unbalanced image may create emphasis, tension, or open space.
- Whether a gaze, gesture, or line of action points toward something important.
- Whether faces, hands, objects, and other essential information remain visible.

Balance does not require symmetry, equal numbers of elements on each side, or centered subjects. Describe the actual visual relationship when a named composition rule is not enough.

# Part 2: Understand how framing can be used

Framing influences what the viewer notices and how subjects relate, but it does not guarantee one emotional interpretation. Context, performance, action, camera distance, focus, movement, and editing all affect the result.

## Possible uses of subject-count framing

- A **single** can focus attention on one person's expression, action, or response. A dirty single can also retain a foreground presence or relationship without making that presence the main subject.
- A **two-shot** can let viewers observe two people together, their interaction, and the space between them without cutting between individual views.
- A **three-shot** can establish a trio or group dynamic and keep shared action in view. It may be clearer than presenting each person separately when their interaction matters.
- A **group shot** can show collective behavior, relative position, or the group's relation to its environment.

These are useful possibilities, not reasons to use a given shot every time a certain number of people appears. Decide whether the relationship or shared action benefits from being visible in the same frame.

## Possible uses of OTS and OTH

OTS can orient the viewer through a foreground shoulder and maintain a sense of connection between a viewpoint and the target. OTH can create a similar layered composition using a hip or lower-body foreground. When subjects occupy different heights or positions, that difference may contribute to a power dynamic, but it does not automatically mean one person has more power.

In a one-person composition, a shoulder or hip can establish where the person is looking from while the viewer sees the object, screen, room, or landscape in front of them. Specify what the viewer should attend to beyond the foreground element.

## Possible uses of POV and inserts

A POV can place viewers inside a character's perception and connect a visible action or object to that character's attention. In a sequence, a look, the viewed subject, and a reaction can clarify the relationship, when the scene benefits from all three beats.

An insert can emphasize a meaningful object, gesture, clue, or action. It can reveal information to the viewer, including information the character has not noticed. Use the detail because it matters, not simply to add a close view.

## Treat framing effects as contextual

Do not translate framing labels into guaranteed meanings such as “OTS always feels intimate” or “an unbalanced frame always feels tense.” State the intended effect in the context of the scene, and select a composition that supports it. When a term does not communicate the image clearly, describe who is visible, what is in the foreground, and what the viewer should focus on.

# Part 3: Adapt framing to social media and AI-generated video

Social media is a distribution context, not one visual style. A talking-head video, tutorial, vlog, product demonstration, short narrative, or group conversation can all benefit from different framing choices. Do not automatically impose a sequence of singles, OTS coverage, inserts, or cinematic cutting patterns.

## Preserve clarity in the intended format

When known, consider aspect ratio, screen size, viewing distance, platform interface, captions, and on-screen graphics. Use these constraints to keep faces, actions, objects, and text readable. They may affect how subjects are arranged, but they do not prescribe which framing type to use.

For vertical or tightly cropped images, check whether multiple people can fit without awkwardly cutting off faces or gestures. In OTS and OTH compositions, keep the foreground shoulder or hip from blocking too much of the featured subject or target. A single-person OTS can work when the foreground person and the object or space they observe form a clear composition.

## Make AI-generated compositions unambiguous

When a framing label alone may produce an unpredictable result, describe the visible arrangement:

- State how many people are **featured**, and separately mention any partial foreground person or body part.
- Identify who or what occupies the foreground and who or what is the focus or target.
- For OTS or OTH, say whether the shoulder or hip belongs to a visible foreground person, and what the viewer sees beyond it.
- For POV, identify whose view the shot represents and what is visible from that position. Include hands or body parts only if intended.
- For an insert, name the exact object, detail, or action to emphasize and preserve its appearance and continuity.
- Keep the gaze, screen direction, subject identity, positions, and object state coherent across adjacent shots when they are meant to continue the same scene.
- Avoid accidental extra people, duplicated body parts, foreground obstructions, unclear targets, or framing that hides essential information.

These are clarity checks, not limits on creative framing. Unusual, partial, asymmetrical, or obscured compositions are valid when they are intentional and the viewer can still understand the relevant visual information.

## Choose the frame for the specific video

Start from the video's purpose, action, number of relevant subjects, and intended viewer experience. Choose whether the image should feature one or several subjects, use a foreground frame, show a character's view, isolate a detail, or combine these approaches. Keep only the framing details that materially help the generated result.

If a stable direct-to-camera composition best supports a social video, use it. If the content benefits from a POV, insert, OTS, group composition, or a more stylized frame, use that instead. Neither plainness nor cinematic complexity should be a default.

## Recommended framing specification

Use this structure when framing details matter. Omit fields that are not relevant.

```text
Framing — [brief purpose]
Featured subjects: [who is the main subject; number of featured people]
Composition type: [single / two-shot / three-shot / group / OTS / OTH / POV / insert, if useful]
Foreground: [person, shoulder, hip, object, or none]
Focus or target: [who or what the viewer should attend to]
Placement and relationships: [positions, spacing, eyelines, gestures, and relevant background]
Format constraints: [aspect ratio, crop, captions, graphics, or visibility needs]
Continuity: [identity, screen direction, gaze, and object details to preserve]
```

## Final quality check

Before delivering a framing direction, verify:

1. The framing term is used accurately and the visible arrangement is understandable.
2. Featured subject count is distinguished from partial or background people in the frame.
3. OTS and OTH do not assume a second featured person; the foreground element and intended target are clear.
4. POV identifies whose view is shown, and inserts identify the detail that matters.
5. The composition suits the video's purpose, action, and format without relying on a fixed social-media recipe.
6. Faces, objects, gestures, and text that matter remain visible and legible.
7. Any unusual or stylized composition is intentional rather than an artifact of vague instructions.
