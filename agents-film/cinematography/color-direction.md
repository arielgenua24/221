# Color Direction for AI Video Creation

## Purpose

This guide teaches the visual language of color before describing how to use it. Read the concepts first, then apply the decision process to the specific video. Color choices should help communicate the video's intent while remaining coherent, legible, and open to creative alternatives.

Do not treat color associations or palette types as fixed formulas. They describe tools and tendencies. Their meaning depends on the story, the image, the surrounding colors, and the viewer's context.

## Part 1: Understand the language of color

### The three basic dimensions

- **Hue** is the color family, such as red, orange, yellow, green, blue, or violet.
- **Saturation** is the intensity or vividness of a color. Highly saturated colors feel more vivid; low saturation moves toward muted or nearly gray.
- **Value** is how light or dark a color appears.

These dimensions work together. Two images can use the same hue but feel very different because one is pale and muted while the other is dark and vivid. When useful, describe all three dimensions rather than naming a hue alone.

### Palette and color relationships

A **palette** is the set and organization of colors used in an image, scene, or larger work. A palette is more than a list of hues: proportion, value, saturation, lighting, materials, and where each color appears all affect the result.

Common palette relationships include:

- **Monochromatic:** Variations of one hue, using different values and saturation levels. This can create visual unity and a concentrated atmosphere without requiring every surface to be the exact same color.
- **Complementary:** Hues positioned opposite one another on a color wheel. Their contrast can create separation, energy, or tension. The effect depends on their relative brightness, saturation, area, and placement.
- **Analogous:** Neighboring hues on a color wheel. They often blend with less color contrast and can create a cohesive, connected impression.
- **Triadic:** Three hues spaced around a color wheel. This can produce a varied, lively palette. A useful arrangement may give one hue the largest role and use the others in smaller roles, but this is a choice, not a rule.
- **Discordant or accent color:** A color deliberately breaks from an established palette or visual pattern. The difference can draw attention to a subject, object, or moment. It is most effective when the break has a reason and remains readable.

These categories can overlap or be modified. A scene may combine relationships, use only a subdued trace of a complementary hue, or move between palette structures. Do not force a scene into a category when a plain visual description is clearer.

### Color roles and visual hierarchy

When it helps clarify a design, describe the role each color plays:

- **Dominant color:** Occupies the most visual area or establishes the overall impression.
- **Supporting color:** Complements the dominant color, separates visual planes, or carries secondary information.
- **Accent color:** Appears selectively to attract attention or mark a meaningful detail.

These are functional roles, not a fixed ratio or a requirement to use exactly three colors. More than one color can share a role, and a restrained palette may need no distinct accent.

### Color as storytelling

Color can contribute to a video in several ways:

- **Mood and tone:** Hue, saturation, and value can contribute to an emotional atmosphere.
- **Attention:** Contrast, saturation, brightness, and isolation can make an element stand out.
- **Unity:** Repeated colors or related hues can connect shots, settings, or sections.
- **Character, object, or theme association:** A recurring color can become associated with something through repetition and context.
- **Change and transition:** A shift in palette, saturation, value, or color balance can accompany a change in setting, perspective, story, or emotional state.

These are possibilities rather than universal meanings. Red does not always mean danger or passion, and blue does not always mean calm or sadness. A color's meaning must be established by the video itself; it can be ironic, ambiguous, culturally specific, or simply aesthetic.

## Part 2: Apply color to the specific video

### Start with the video's intent

Before selecting colors, understand the material being created:

- What is the subject, purpose, tone, and intended audience?
- What should viewers notice, understand, or feel?
- Which people, objects, locations, actions, or text must remain easy to recognize?
- Does the video have distinct scenes, character perspectives, stages, or emotional changes?
- Are there brand colors, reference images, product colors, wardrobe requirements, or other constraints?
- What platform, aspect ratio, and viewing conditions are known?

Do not assume that every video needs a distinctive cinematic palette. A naturalistic look, an existing brand palette, or faithful product and skin colors may matter more than expressive color design for a particular request.

### Explore suitable palette directions

Use color relationships as a vocabulary for exploration, not a checklist. Consider the palette structures and color roles that could serve the video's intent. Choose one direction when the brief is clear. When the choice would meaningfully affect the creative direction and the brief allows it, consider presenting a small number of distinct palette candidates with a short explanation of what each would contribute.

The agent may use different palettes in different scenes or stages of a video when a change helps communicate the content. A video does not have to use one palette throughout. It may also keep a consistent palette across all scenes when unity is the stronger goal. Make the choice based on the video, not on a universal preference for consistency or change.

### Explain the visual intention

For a significant color decision, be able to state:

1. What the color treatment is intended to communicate or emphasize.
2. Which palette relationship or visual pattern is being considered, if it has a useful name.
3. Which colors or color families dominate, support, or act as accents, when those roles matter.
4. How hue, saturation, and value contribute to the look.
5. Where the treatment begins, changes, or ends across shots or scenes, when relevant.

Do not add color theory terminology to a final prompt when it makes the instruction less clear. Translate the intention into visible properties, such as warm amber light against a cool blue background, muted green surroundings with one saturated red object, or a gradual shift from pale low-contrast colors to darker, more saturated tones.

### Maintain coherence while preserving useful differences

Across a sequence, keep color relationships intentional. Check whether recurring subjects, locations, wardrobe, props, and lighting remain consistent when they are meant to. If colors change, decide whether the change is a deliberate story cue or an unwanted continuity drift.

Coherence does not mean every frame must match. A shot may depart from the established palette to draw attention, distinguish a new space, or mark a transition. Make the reason for a noticeable departure clear.

For AI video generation, describe the visible result and preserve critical color constraints across shots. Avoid vague directions such as “make it cinematic” when a concrete description of palette, contrast, saturation, value, lighting, or color placement is possible. Avoid overly precise color specifications when they conflict with the subject, the references, or the desired naturalism.

### Check the generated result

Review the result in context, not only as a palette description. Check that:

- Important subjects and actions remain legible against their backgrounds.
- Color contrast directs attention as intended without obscuring meaningful details.
- Faces, skin, products, wardrobe, text, and brand elements retain the required appearance.
- The color treatment is reasonably consistent across shots where continuity matters.
- Any strong color break or palette transition reads as intentional.
- The palette supports the video rather than overpowering its subject or purpose.

If the result does not serve the intended experience, revise the color direction. Do not preserve a palette choice merely because it has a recognized theoretical label.

## Recommended color-direction specification

Use this structure when color is important to the video. Omit fields that do not matter.

```text
Color direction — [brief intent]
Purpose: [what the color should communicate, emphasize, or distinguish]
Palette direction: [one or more palette relationships, or a plain-language description]
Color roles: [dominant / supporting / accent colors, if useful]
Hue, saturation, and value: [visible qualities that matter]
Scene or sequence behavior: [where the palette remains consistent or changes, and why]
Constraints: [colors, subjects, text, products, references, or continuity details to preserve]
```

## Final quality check

Before delivering a color direction, verify:

1. The color concepts are used accurately, but terminology is included only when it clarifies the visual instruction.
2. The proposed color treatment follows from the video's purpose, subject, tone, and constraints.
3. Palette categories and color associations are treated as options and contextual tendencies, not rules.
4. The video may use one palette or several, including changes between scenes, when that choice serves the content.
5. The description communicates observable color relationships rather than relying only on abstract labels.
6. Important subjects and information remain legible and visually coherent.

## Adapting Color Direction to Social Media Videos

Social media is a distribution context, not a color style. A vlog, TikTok, YouTube video, product demonstration, or short documentary does not automatically require brighter colors, stronger contrast, higher saturation, or a single consistent palette.

Consider the platform, aspect ratio, screen size, viewing conditions, and any relevant brand or content requirements when they are known. Use those constraints to keep important subjects, products, actions, and text visually clear. Do not let them dictate the palette when the video's subject, tone, or story calls for a different treatment.

Choose color direction for the specific video. A restrained or naturalistic palette may serve one video; a vivid, high-contrast, monochromatic, or changing palette may serve another. The agent may consider different palette directions or use different palettes across scenes when that helps communicate the content. Treat these as options to evaluate, not social-media defaults.
