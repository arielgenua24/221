# The Seven Visual Components for AI Video Creation

## Purpose

Use this guide to describe and plan a video's visual structure. A frame is built from more than camera angle or the rule of thirds: space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, and visual rhythm can each be observed and adjusted. Learn them separately, then choose the few that best serve the story or viewing experience.

# Part 1: Understand the seven components

## Space

**Space** is the impression of depth and spatial relationships within the frame. Deep space uses cues such as converging lines, size differences, overlap, texture change, and movement toward or away from the camera. Flat space emphasizes the picture plane with frontal staging, fewer depth cues, and movement across the frame. Limited space layers relatively flat planes. Ambiguous space withholds reliable size or position cues. Describe the intended viewer experience rather than treating any one type as inherently better.

## Line

**Line** can come from an object edge, a tonal boundary, architecture, a gaze, or the path of movement. Notice its orientation (horizontal, vertical, diagonal), direction, and quality (straight or curved). Reduce a frame to its dominant lines to see where they lead attention and whether neighboring shots share a visual motif.

## Shape

**Shape** is the outline or mass formed by lines and contrast. It can be the silhouette of a person, product, prop, shadow, or group of objects. Circles, triangles, and rectangles are useful simplified descriptions; real objects can combine them. Judge shape in the final crop, where an object may read differently than it does in isolation.

## Tone

**Tone** is the distribution of light and dark. Lighting, exposure, and the brightness of sets, clothing, or objects can make an area distinct or conceal it. Check the image in grayscale to see whether the subject remains readable. A bright region can attract attention, but its importance depends on size, contrast, faces, motion, and the rest of the frame.

## Color

**Color** concerns hue, saturation, value, placement, and change across shots. It can connect people or places, distinguish sections, or draw attention to a detail. Do not assign fixed meanings to hues: establish any association through context and repetition. Use the existing [color direction guide](../cinematography/color-direction.md) when a palette needs detailed planning.

## Movement

**Movement** includes subjects, objects, camera, and the viewer's shifting point of attention. A subject crossing a static frame differs from a camera tracking alongside it; a push through foreground and background can change the sense of depth. Identify what moves, in which direction, at what pace, and relative to what remains still. Use the existing [camera movement guide](../cinematography/camera-movement.md) for camera vocabulary.

## Visual rhythm

**Visual rhythm** is the pattern of repetition, alternation, and change in images over time. Repeated windows, alternating colors, a recurring gesture, starts and stops within a shot, or the spacing of cuts can create rhythm. It exists even without music. Distinguish it from the musical beat described in the [rhythm and music guides](../ritm-and-music/beat-and-waveform.md).

# Part 2: Build a visual progression

## Start with the idea, then choose components

Name what changes for the viewer: understanding, anticipation, intimacy, scale, tension, relief, or attention. Compare the beginning, turning point, and ending of a sequence. Decide which component or small combination can express that change clearly. A cramped space becoming deep, a repeated circular shape being broken by a sharp silhouette, or a static frame giving way to movement can make a progression visible.

**Affinity** means similarity between visual choices; **contrast** means difference. Affinity can establish continuity or a recognizable style. Contrast can mark a change or create emphasis. Neither guarantees a particular emotion. A strong change has more impact when the audience has first learned the pattern it interrupts, and when the interruption corresponds to something meaningful in the content.

Build a simple visual plan:

1. Identify the story or communication goal of each section.
2. Describe the current visual pattern using the seven components.
3. Select one or two components to hold relatively stable, and one or two to change at important moments.
4. Specify the observable change in framing, blocking, light, color, or edit timing.
5. Review the sequence in order, rather than approving each frame in isolation.

For example, a product demonstration can begin with flat, clear frontal views for comprehension and shift to deeper space and more subject movement when showing the product in use. That is one possible strategy, not a template. A quiet documentary may benefit from the opposite progression or no deliberate intensification at all.

# Part 3: Adapt visual structure to social media and AI-generated video

The platform and aspect ratio affect which relationships stay visible. In a narrow vertical crop, a diagonal may dominate, a product silhouette may be lost behind text, and depth cues can disappear if the background is too soft. Check the actual crop at phone size. Make faces, actions, products, and essential text legible before adding visual complexity.

For short social videos, a visual pattern can be established quickly through repeated framing, color, line direction, or movement. Change it when the content changes: a reveal, comparison, punchline, or transition to a new stage. Do not force all seven components to change together. For a talking-head explanation, stable space and tone may support clarity; movement or shape can carry the occasional demonstration. For a fashion reel, silhouette, fabric motion, line, and color may matter more than detailed spatial geography.

For generated footage, state the visible relationships concretely. “Deep space with the model walking toward camera between converging racks” is easier to execute and review than “cinematic depth.” Preserve recurring subjects, wardrobe, props, and intentional visual motifs across generated shots. If the model cannot reliably produce a planned change, simplify the composition or create it in the edit.

## Recommended visual-structure specification

```text
Sequence purpose: [what the viewer should understand or feel]
Established pattern: [which components stay consistent]
Key change: [which component changes, when, and why]
Shot-level evidence: [observable space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, or rhythm]
Format constraints: [crop, text, product visibility, continuity]
Review question: [how to tell whether the progression is readable]
```

## Final quality check

1. Can the important visual choices be described in observable terms?
2. Does the selected pattern support the content rather than add decoration?
3. Does a major contrast occur where the story or message changes?
4. Are visual relationships preserved across the actual delivery crop?
5. Is the result understandable without assuming that every viewer shares one color or shape association?
