# Beat and Waveform for Video Editing

## Purpose

Use this guide to locate useful musical moments and decide whether an image should change there. Learn to listen and count before using a waveform or an automatic grid. The goal is an edit that expresses the video's idea, not a cut on every visible spike.

# Part 1: Understand musical rhythm

## Pulse, beat, bar, and subdivision

- **Pulse / beat:** The recurring unit a listener can tap along to. It may be implied even when no drum sounds on it.
- **Tempo:** The rate of the pulse, commonly described in beats per minute (BPM). A faster tempo does not automatically require shorter shots.
- **Bar / measure:** A recurring group of beats. In common 4/4 music, count `1, 2, 3, 4` and repeat; the `1` is the downbeat of the next bar. Do not assume every track is in 4/4.
- **Subdivision:** A smaller rhythmic position between beats. In 4/4, eighth notes can be counted `1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &`; sixteenth notes, `1 e & a 2 e & a ...`. Use only the subdivision that you can hear reliably.
- **Syncopation / offbeat:** An accent between expected strong beats. It can make an edit feel surprising without speeding up every shot.
- **Attack / transient:** The onset of a sound, often visible as a sharp waveform change. It may be a kick, snare, clap, instrument, or vocal consonant. A transient is a candidate cue, not proof of the underlying beat.

The waveform shows amplitude over time. A prominent peak can help locate an audible attack, but a compressed track can hide it, and a loud sound can occur off the beat. Listen while counting; use the waveform to refine the timing rather than treating every peak as an instruction to cut.

## Read the music at more than one scale

First hear the track's broad sections: introduction, verse, build, chorus, breakdown, ending, or whatever structure it actually has. Then count bars and beats within a relevant section. Finally inspect individual attacks or subdivisions near the moment you want to synchronize. A section change often matters more to the story than a routine drum hit.

When timing a visible event, distinguish its **preparation** from its **impact**: a person bends before jumping, a hand starts turning before a garment swings, and a product is lifted before it lands. Align the perceptual impact with the chosen musical cue when that relationship serves the moment. Place the clip's in-point early enough for the action to be legible.

# Part 2: Decide where rhythm helps the edit

## A practical listening pass

1. State the video's intended feeling or progression before selecting cut points.
2. Listen once without editing. Note the large changes in energy, instrumentation, silence, or melody.
3. Count the pulse aloud or tap along. Check where bars begin; do not force a count that drifts away from the music.
4. Mark only the attacks, offbeats, or section changes that could serve a reveal, movement, line, or transition.
5. Place the footage. Choose among a cut, a visible action, a text change, a camera move, or a held shot at each selected cue.
6. Play the result at normal speed. If the cut feels late or early, adjust by listening and watching, not by trusting marker placement alone.

Use markers as editable suggestions. FilmDaft describes marking beats or offbeats while listening and then placing footage against those markers. It also notes that rigidly cutting at the same position in every bar becomes predictable. Vary the pattern when the image and story benefit: hold through an expected beat, cut on a subdivision before the downbeat, or let a moment resolve after it.

## Examples of reasoning

- **Reveal:** The music builds over two bars. Hold the closed box during the build; reveal its contents at a strong section change. The reveal is the reason for the alignment.
- **Human reaction:** A laugh or surprised look begins just after a musical hit. Preserve the reaction's readable duration instead of trimming it to the next beat.
- **Detailed action:** A hand opens a clasp between main beats. An offbeat or subdivision may fit the click better than a large downbeat.

# Part 3: Apply rhythm to social media and AI-generated video

Short-form video does not require every shot to be beat-synced. A tutorial may need longer holds for comprehension; a fashion montage may have more musical movement; a personal story may use music only beneath the voice. Choose the treatment from the format, subject, audience, and desired experience.

For product or clothing footage, identify the exact event to synchronize: a heel landing, turn reaching its widest silhouette, fabric flick reaching its peak, or a bag touching a surface. Give the action enough lead-in that the viewer understands what moved. If source footage cannot reach the cue naturally, choose a different beat, retime modestly if acceptable, or use another take. Avoid forcing implausible motion merely to hit a marker.

If an agent has audio-analysis tools, it can propose tempo, beat positions, transients, and section boundaries. Treat these as estimates and validate them by listening, especially with syncopation, rubato, silence, or heavily compressed tracks. If there is no audio or no tool to analyze it, do not invent exact timestamps or BPM. Ask for the track when exact synchronization is required; otherwise give a relative plan such as “the turn lands on the first strong hit after the build.”

## Recommended rhythm specification

```text
Moment: [event or story purpose]
Musical cue: [section change / bar downbeat / beat / subdivision / specific attack]
Visual treatment: [cut / action peak / text / camera move / hold]
Lead-in and aftermath: [what must be visible before and after the cue]
Timing confidence: [measured timestamp, approximate position, or relative cue]
Reason: [what the synchronization communicates]
```

## Final quality check

1. Is the pulse counted by listening rather than inferred from waveform peaks alone?
2. Are the chosen cues meaningful to the visual action or story?
3. Does the viewer have enough time to recognize preparation, impact, and consequence?
4. Are holds and offbeat choices allowed when they work better than routine downbeat cuts?
5. Are precise timing claims supported by the actual audio?
