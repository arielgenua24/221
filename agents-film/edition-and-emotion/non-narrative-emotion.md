# Editing Non-Narrative Video for Emotion

## Purpose

Use this guide for ads, explainers, product videos, testimonials, montages, and other pieces without a conventional film plot. The edit still needs an emotional progression. Information becomes more memorable when the viewer can see why it matters to someone.

# Part 1: Understand the theory

Walter Murch's six priorities still offer useful questions outside narrative cinema: emotion, story, rhythm, eye trace, screen direction, and physical space. Here, **story** can mean the progression of an idea or experience rather than a fictional plot. A product demonstration, for instance, can move from a recognizable difficulty to an action and then a visible result.

The No Film School article applies Murch's framework to company reels and social video. Its author argues that rhythm, music, and on-screen text often matter more than strict spatial continuity in a non-narrative montage. This is a context-specific adaptation, not a reason to discard space in every ad or explainer. If the viewer must understand how an object is used, where a person is looking, or what changed between before and after, continuity is essential.

Emotion and information reinforce each other. An unsupported claim such as "this saves time" provides information; showing a person recover time for something they value gives the claim felt consequence. Do not claim a result the footage cannot honestly support.

# Part 2: Build an emotional arc from real material

## Define the viewer's starting and ending states

Write a one-sentence progression: "The viewer starts [feeling or belief] and ends [feeling or belief] because they witness [evidence]." Examples include uncertainty to confidence, frustration to relief, curiosity to understanding, or distance to identification. Do not assume every brand video must be exciting.

Choose a small number of beats that can carry the progression:

1. **Recognition:** A detail, question, or situation gives the viewer a reason to care.
2. **Development:** Actions, contrasts, or testimony deepen the issue or show the process.
3. **Payoff:** A visible consequence, answer, or human response completes the thought.

These beats may be very short or may occupy an entire piece. Their job is to make the order purposeful.

## Select footage by meaning

Label source clips by what they show and what they might make the viewer feel. Prefer observable actions and specific details over generic smiling faces or attractive filler. A candid hesitation, a failed attempt, or a small response may convey more than a polished hero shot.

For a testimonial, preserve the speaker's meaning and timing. Do not rearrange words or reactions to imply an endorsement, result, or causal chain that did not occur. For a generated scene, write the causal chain explicitly so its actions and reactions can be checked.

## Let sound and silence carry part of the edit

Music should support the intended tone and turning points. Consider speech, ambient sound, a breath, a click, or silence before adding a track. A sound bridge can connect shots; a sudden drop can focus attention. Test the edit without music to see whether the visual sequence still communicates its basic point. Then test with sound off and captions visible for contexts where viewers may watch muted.

## Control pace and attention

Shorten a shot when its point is already clear; hold it when the viewer needs to recognize, compare, or feel its consequence. Faster cutting is one possible way to create energy, but speed can erase a product step, joke setup, or emotional reaction. Keep captions and labels in predictable, legible positions when they need to be read. Match the visual beat to the time required to read them.

# Part 3: Apply the method to social media and AI-generated video

Start from the particular format and promise of the video. A quick demonstration should make the problem and result easy to compare. A founder story may need room for a human pause. A montage may rely on cumulative contrast. Use the platform's dimensions and likely viewing context to check whether expressions, objects, and text remain legible; do not impose one editing speed on all social content.

If AI generates the footage, ask for clips that can be edited together: a clear beginning and end to actions, consistent subject and prop details, and separate reaction or insert shots where the emotional turn occurs. Generate enough visual evidence for the claim. If the result is not visible, revise the concept or footage rather than relying on a caption to assert it.

### Example: a short product demonstration

- **Recognition:** Show the user's repeated difficulty in one concrete action; let the failed attempt finish.
- **Development:** Show the product solving the same action from a comparable viewpoint, with the key step visible.
- **Payoff:** Hold long enough to see the result and a credible human response. Cut to a product detail only if it clarifies the result.

The editing choice is governed by what the viewer must notice, not by a fixed shot count. If the two attempts are not comparable, the edit may feel persuasive but fail to demonstrate the claimed change.

## Recommended edit brief

```text
Video purpose and audience: [who it serves and why]
Starting -> ending viewer state: [emotional or belief shift]
Recognition / development / payoff: [observable beats]
Evidence required: [what the footage must actually show]
Sound role: [speech, ambience, music, silence]
Pace and reading time: [where to accelerate or hold]
Attention path: [subject and text placement across cuts]
Continuity needs: [which spatial and action details matter]
```

## Final quality check

1. Does the sequence create a meaningful change in feeling or understanding?
2. Does the footage support every implied claim?
3. Can the viewer read the action, text, and result at the chosen pace?
4. Do sound and music strengthen a moment that already has visual meaning?
5. Are omitted spatial details harmless for this specific format?
6. Does the ending pay off what the opening asked the viewer to care about?
