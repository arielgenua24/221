# The Rule of Six for Emotion-Led Editing

## Purpose

Use this guide when choosing the order and timing of shots. A cut should change what the viewer feels, understands, or notices. Start with the intended experience, then decide how much visual continuity that experience needs.

# Part 1: Understand the theory

Walter Murch's Rule of Six ranks six considerations for a cut:

1. **Emotion:** Does the cut preserve or change the feeling at the right moment?
2. **Story:** Does it advance the viewer's understanding of what matters?
3. **Rhythm:** Does it arrive at a pace that supports that feeling and meaning?
4. **Eye trace:** Can the viewer find the important subject after the cut?
5. **Two-dimensional screen direction:** Are left/right relationships and movement across the frame coherent?
6. **Three-dimensional space:** Can the viewer understand where people and objects are in the scene?

Murch's commonly cited weighting is 51%, 23%, 10%, 7%, 5%, and 4% respectively. Treat these as a **priority order and a way to discuss tradeoffs**, not as a measurable scoring formula. Emotion comes first, but a viewer who cannot tell what happened may not feel the intended emotion.

The relevant unit is not an isolated shot. It is the relationship between the outgoing shot, the incoming shot, and the moment of transition. Ask what the viewer carries across that boundary: a question, a glance, a movement, a sound, or an expectation.

# Part 2: Decide where and why to cut

## Start with the emotional beat

Name the feeling before adjusting timing: anticipation, relief, intimacy, dread, pride, amusement, or another specific response. Then identify the visible or audible event that could produce it. "Make it emotional" is too vague to edit against.

- **Hold** when the viewer needs time to read a reaction, notice a detail, or anticipate an action.
- **Cut** when the new shot changes the meaning, answers a question, or raises the stakes.
- **Cut earlier** when withholding the end of an action creates useful tension or momentum.
- **Cut later** when the consequence or human response matters more than the action itself.

Do not equate emotion with a close-up, dramatic music, or fast cutting. A wide shot can make isolation palpable; a quiet pause can make a reveal land. The meaning depends on context.

## Make the story legible

For each cut, identify what the viewer knows before and after it. Remove repeated information unless repetition changes its meaning. If a shot is beautiful but interrupts the emotional argument, move or remove it.

When emotion and spatial continuity conflict, a small continuity shortcut can be acceptable if the action stays intelligible. Preserve the details that matter to comprehension: identity, object state, direction of action, and cause and effect. Deliberate disorientation needs a reason and an eventual point of orientation.

## Shape rhythm beyond the music beat

Rhythm comes from shot duration, action within the shot, speech, silence, and sound changes. A cut on every musical beat may become predictable. A pause before a reveal, a held reaction after it, or a cut against the beat may better serve the moment. Test the sequence once with sound and once muted to separate visual meaning from music's contribution.

## Guide the eye across the cut

Note where attention sits at the end of shot A and where the crucial information appears at the start of shot B. Keep it easy to find when speed or clarity matters. Move attention deliberately when a search or surprise is intended. Text overlays, faces, and product details all compete for attention.

## Respect space when the action needs it

Screen direction and geography are practical tools, not absolute rules. A physical demonstration, dialogue exchange, or before/after comparison often needs consistent orientation. A memory montage may tolerate gaps or jumps. Review the sequence as a viewer encountering it for the first time.

# Part 3: Adapt the rule to social media and AI-generated video

Social videos vary: a personal story, product demonstration, comedic sketch, and direct explanation need different pacing. Identify the video's purpose, audience, tone, duration, aspect ratio, and viewing conditions before building the edit. The opening should establish a meaningful question, feeling, or event promptly; a fixed number of seconds or cuts is not a universal rule.

For generated footage, plan the transitions before generating every shot. Request the coverage needed for an emotional beat: the action, its consequence, a reaction, and an orienting view where necessary. A generated character with restrained expression can still work when the surrounding shots establish what they see and what is at stake. Editing can add context; it cannot make a contradictory expression or impossible action coherent by itself.

Check identity, clothing, props, lighting, eyelines, and object state at every adjacent cut. Where AI footage fails continuity, use a motivated insert, a change of viewpoint, sound bridge, or a re-generated shot. Do not hide a material contradiction behind speed.

### Example: a customer finally opens a delayed package

1. **Before:** Hold on the unopened package and the customer's hesitation. Intended feeling: anticipation.
2. **Cut to action:** Show hands breaking the seal. Story moves forward; the package's position stays coherent.
3. **Withhold briefly:** Stay on the customer's face as they see inside, before showing the contents. Intended feeling: curiosity shared with the customer.
4. **Reveal:** Show the item from their viewpoint, then a response that fits the reveal. Intended feeling: relief or delight.

If the opening gesture is already expressive, do not add extra cuts just for pace.


## Final quality check

1. Can the intended emotional change be named for each important cut?
2. Does each cut reveal, withhold, or reframe something meaningful?
3. Does the sequence remain understandable without explanatory captions?
4. Does the rhythm follow the emotional arc rather than an arbitrary cut frequency?
5. Are the important subjects easy to find after each cut?
6. Are any continuity breaks intentional and readable?
